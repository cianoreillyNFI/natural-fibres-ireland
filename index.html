Carbon

Carbon, farming & fibre

Natural fibres capture biogenic carbon as they grow. What happens next determines whether that carbon is quickly returned, stored for decades, or potentially certified under a recognised methodology.

The important distinction

A plant absorbing CO₂ is not the same thing as a carbon credit.

EU CRCF

The European framework

The EU Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming (CRCF) Regulation creates a voluntary certification framework for permanent carbon removals, carbon farming and carbon storage in products.

To qualify, activities must use an applicable methodology and meet rules around quantification, additionality, storage, sustainability and independent verification.

EU CRCF overview ↗ Read Regulation (EU) 2024/3012 ↗

Three routes worth understanding

Land

Carbon farming

In July 2026 the Commission adopted methodologies covering agriculture and agroforestry on mineral soils, rewetting and restoration of peatlands and other organic soils, and afforestation.

A hemp or flax crop may form part of a qualifying farming system, but the crop itself is not an automatic credit.

Products

Long-lived biobased materials

The CRCF Regulation covers carbon stored for at least 35 years in long-lasting products. The Commission specifically identifies bio-based construction products from timber and agricultural crops as an area of potential.

A dedicated methodology for carbon storage in buildings is planned for the end of 2026.

Residues

Biochar

Biochar Carbon Removal already has an EU methodology. That may become relevant where qualifying fibre or woody residues enter a compliant pyrolysis and storage pathway.

Feedstock, process emissions, permanence and monitoring still matter.

IRELAND · MAY 2026

What does this mean here?

DAFM is developing a National Carbon Farming Framework. The Department published the report from its consultation on the key principles in May 2026.

The direction is clear: verified carbon farming could become an additional income stream for farmers and land managers. The details still matter — methodologies, monitoring costs, contracts, ownership of claims, market demand and price.

For Irish natural fibres, the opportunity is likely to sit in a combination of land management, traceability, long-lived products and residue pathways, rather than a simple “tonnes grown = credits earned” calculation.
